ABOUT THE SPECIMEN

The Blue Whale of Colombo

The blue whale skeleton housed in the Colombo National Museum is one of the most remarkable natural history specimens in South Asia. Measuring approximately 27 metres in length and estimated to have weighed over 100 tonnes in life, this specimen was acquired in 1914 following a stranding along the Sri Lankan coast. Over a century later, it remains the centrepiece of the museum's natural history collection — a monument to the ocean's most extraordinary creature.

The skeleton was painstakingly cleaned, articulated, and suspended by the museum's curators over several years. Its bones — bleached pale by time — retain the characteristic shape of the largest animal species ever known to have existed on Earth. The skull alone spans nearly 6 metres, while the elongated ribcage speaks to the immense lung capacity required to sustain a creature that breathes air yet lives entirely beneath the sea.

Unknown Facts About the Bones

What many visitors do not realise is that blue whale bones are not solid — they are filled with oil-saturated spongy tissue, an adaptation that helps the living whale regulate buoyancy. This makes the skeleton extraordinarily lightweight relative to its immense size. The fossilised oil content is what gives aged blue whale bones their distinctive yellowish hue over time.

The vertebral column contains over 60 individual vertebrae, many as wide as a dining table. Each one bears the compression marks of carrying a body heavier than any land animal could support. The flippers — five-fingered beneath the skin — tell the evolutionary story of a land mammal that returned to the sea over 50 million years ago.

Myths & Findings

For generations, local fishermen along Sri Lanka's southern coast held beliefs that stranded blue whales were signs of abundance — that where a giant fell, the sea would give generously for years to come. The 1914 stranding near Mirissa became part of regional folklore long before the specimen reached the museum.

Modern research has revealed that blue whale strandings are often linked to sonar interference or changes in ocean temperature. Studies of the Colombo specimen have contributed to understanding of 19th-century Indian Ocean whale populations — bone density analysis suggests this individual was approximately 70–80 years of age at the time of death.
